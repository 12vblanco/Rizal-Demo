Rizal is famously known for his last literary work Mi Ultimo Adios (My Last Farewell) which he wrote on the day of his execution. But unbeknownst to many, apart from possibly having a natural talent with rhymes, Rizal’s genius in writing poetry is highly influenced by her mother Doña Teodora and the sessions on reading literary pieces and reciting poems at the Ateneo Municipal (Villaroel 1984, 138-141). Craig (1913, 107) narrates how Rizal brings his compositions to Calamba for his mother to critique, and how the works of Romantic poets like Zorilla (who wrote themes on patriotism by looking back on Spain’s history) influenced his own works like the Junta al Pasig, a zarzuela he wrote while he was studying at the University of Santo Tomas.

His love for poetry continued during his studies at the University of Santo Tomas wherein he also gained poet friends such as Fr. Evaristo F. Arias, who possibly became one of his models in the university despite not having the same literary academy in place like the Ateneo. In his second year of Medicine, he was encouraged to join the first literary contest organized by the Liceo Artistico - Literario de Manila (Lyceum of Art and Literature) in 1879 and submitted his poem titled A la Juventud Filipina (To the Filipino Youth) (Villaroel 1984, 143-145).

The ten-stanza poem is an ode to the Filipino youth whom he called bella esperanza de la patria mia (beautiful hope of my country). Uplifting their spirits, he describes the genius that his fellow young Filipinos naturally have—in the arts and sciences—and invites them to untie the heavy chains and unleash their creativity. Some critics called this an act of bravery. Firstly, he refers to the Philippines as his homeland instead of Spain especially during that time, and secondly, he tries to ignite the innate power, purity, and curiosity of the Filipino youth. He also boosts their esteem by placing his confidence in their potential.

Rizal, at 18, already had a powerful play with words that convinced the judges to give him the first prize in the natural-born Filipinos and Mestizos section where he earned a diploma of honor and a feather-shaped quill made of silver with a gold ribbon on it.

While there are people interpreting this work of Rizal to be more nationalistic rather than a simple spark of inspiration among the youth, Villaroel (1984, 146) thinks that it just expresses Rizal’s joy because the young early Filipinos were finally included, although in a separate section or category, in a literary contest organized and judged by the Spaniards. This is evident in the lines ‘Alza su tersa frente, Juventud Filipina, en este día!’ (Raise your smooth forehead, Filipino Youth, on this day!) and ‘Dia, dia felice, Filipinas gentil, para tu suelo!’ (Day, happy day, Gentle Philippines, for your soil!).

Rizal’s poetic words in A la Juventud Filipina provokes a sense of hope and encouragement to the youth, which he carried through his life to the rest of the Filipino people even until his death, leaving his last sweet verses, still for his motherland.
