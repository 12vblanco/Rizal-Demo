Germany, or Deutschland to its citizens, played an important role in the life of our hero Dr. Jose Rizal. It became his second home in Europe during the time when it was challenging for an extranjero like him to adapt to and familiarize with the way of living in another country. This new feeling made Rizal feel like a newborn child as he is going to learn a lot of new things before his eyes, and will unveil a side of him that will leave his countrymen in awe.

Whenever we hear Jose Rizal, the first thing we think of are his great novels Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo. But aside from these, a place in Germany occasionally connects to many of us when Rizal is mentioned—-Heidelberg. It is the fifth-largest city in Germany and houses the university of its namesake where Rizal took his ophthalmology studies. The city’s Baroque-style architecture, scenic view of plains and mountains from afar, and the beautiful riverwalk made Rizal both fall in love with this city and long for his hometown. This feeling led him to write a masterpiece called A las flores de Heidelberg. Rizal took his ophthalmology studies in Heidelberg in 1886, under the tutelage of Dr. Otto Becker. Rizal also chose to study his specialization in ophthalmology here because of the university’s advanced tools and technology, which became an additional force to his motivation—to cure his mother’s vision threatened by a cataract.

Rizal’s encounter with Karl Ullmer was nothing short of serendipitous. While walking on the pavements of Heidelberg, Rizal had a good conversation with Ullmer’s wife, which led him to meeting Pastor Karl. The simple conversation ended up with Rizal being a tenant at the Ullmer residence in Wilhemsfeld, a hilly municipality some 10 kilometers from Heidelberg. Apparently, Rizal chose Wilhelmsfeld for his literary work because of the lower cost for accommodation and quieter environment. Rizal’s stay with the Ullmers drew him closer to the family. He also became friends with Karl’s son, Friedrich, and the Ullmers also helped him study German. During his stay in Wilhelmsfeld, Rizal finished the final chapters of Noli Me Tangere before its publication in Berlin and improved his German vocabulary, which he later used for translating some well-known Western literatures such as the screenplay of Wilhelm Tell and the five tales of Hans Christian Andersen, which the latter was written by Rizal based on his observations and experiences of European culture.

Rizal’s stay in Germany has also unveiled his artistic and funny side. Even a serious person has their own funny side, one that we do not see or hear often. Before his renowned sculptures such as The Triumph of Science over Death and Mother’s Revenge, he has done several artworks and sketches during his time as a traveler. Rizal used to sketch portraits and landscapes on his notebook, even on a small piece of paper. Of his artworks and sketches, one, if not, of the most recognized is The Baptism of Two Brothers. Rizal drew inspiration for this drawing from the German cartoon Max and Moritz and reflected the more comical side of Rizal. Another one is a cartoon sketch of a man farting, which the Ullmer household found very funny. Rizal being a funny man through his sketches is something worth taking interest in. Aside from these funny sketches, Rizal used to draw portraits of the Ullmers during his free time.

Rizal made some of the sketches sometime in 1886, and are part of the Ullmer Collection. The Ullmers kept all of the letters and personal belongings Rizal left. They kept everything in a special box, which was inherited by Fritz and Hans, the grandsons of Karl Ullmer. The said box was later donated to the National Library of the Philippines sometime in 1961. Some of its contents are now currently displayed at the library’s Permanent Gallery for free public viewing. The collection also includes a copy of the first edition of Noli Me Tangere signed by Rizal himself as a gift of gratitude to the Ullmers. Remember the auction of the same first edition at the Leon Gallery in 2025 that amounted to 19 million pesos? The Library possessed the same first edition copy. The only difference is that Rizal signed the copy for the one who let him live and treated him like a family during one of the most difficult times of his life.

On September 4, 2025, the National Library of the Philippines, together with the Office of Senator Loren Legarda and the Philippines-Guest of Honor Committee at Frankfurter Buchmesse (Frankfurt Bookfair) launched an exhibit featuring Rizal and Germany. The exhibit mainly showcased the entire Ullmer Collection, and the significant connection between our national hero and Germany. The exhibit was curated by commissioner Lisa Guerrero-Nakpil of the National Historical Commission of the Philippines, which ran until October 17, 2025. The said exhibit was visited by hundreds of visitors including students, historians, researchers, and cultural workers.

We may know Rizal as an intelligent person, jack of all trades, and a romantic person, just like how he was portrayed before us. But it is important to know that, like us, he was just a human being. Knowing the artistic and literary side of Rizal is vital for knowing who he is as a person. We may not be Rizal, but we can be like him in our own ways.
