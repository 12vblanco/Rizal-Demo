Even before studying in Manila, the young Pepe had already shown an interest in sculpting. He started making models of birds and butterflies made of clay and wax as play time in his childhood home in Calamba. He eventually carved with a pen knife despite no formal training.

Rizal used the same material to carve what would become an extant manifestation of his artistic talent more than a century after his death. At the request of his Jesuit professor in Ateneo Municipal, Father Jose Leonard, Rizal carved an image of the Sacred Heart of Jesus out of batikuling (Litsea leytensis Merr.) wood.

His training in Ateneo Municipal honed his talent in sculpting. Here, his formative years were deeply immersed in the arts and humanities. While his primary subjects were religious studies, mathematics, and the sciences, Rizal took what they call classes de adorno, or cultural studies. These encompass music, painting, and sculpture. His teacher in sculpture was Romualdo de Jesus, a Filipino.

Father Leonard took notice of Rizal’s talent in this artistic field after seeing an image of the Virgin Mary which Rizal sculpted. As such, he commissioned Rizal to sculpt the image of the Sacred Heart of Jesus. This nine-inch statuette depicts life-like details and proportions, indicating Rizal’s mastery despite his youth. Some of the intricate features of Rizal’s Sacred Heart image were the strands of Jesus Christ’s hair, the folds of his garment, and the hand clasping the Sacred Heart to his chest.

The material which he used, batikuling, was commonly used by wood carvers of Paete, Laguna because of its cream color, grain/texture, and appropriate hardness that allows the carver to easily manipulate their carving tools through the wood. Batikuling is an endemic species in the Philippines that is also preferred because of its longevity and resistance to termites, woodborers, and fungi.

Father Leonard supposedly intended to bring the statue to Spain. Nonetheless, the Jesuit fathers of Ateneo Municipal kept and displayed it in the dormitory, where it served as an object for inspiration of the young students then. Literary figure and historian Nick Joaquin noted the irony of enshrining Rizal’s work as the doctor was barred from entering the school, during his first return to the Philippines from Europe. This prohibition stemmed from distancing from the Roman Catholic religion. Until present, the Ateneo Municipal, now the Ateneo de Manila University, keeps the original image for preservation, having survived the tumult and destruction of Ateneo’s former site in Manila during the Second World War.

Rizal requested to see this image of the Sacred Heart of Jesus shortly before his death, after asking the chaplain if Ateneo Municipal still possessed the image. It was brought to his cell in Fort Santiago several days before his death. Whatever the debates regarding Rizal’s religious views and beliefs near the time of his martyrdom, the Sacred Heart of Jesus was a companion to Rizal on the days and nights of his incarceration, standing on his table as he was writing his farewell to the motherland and his loved ones in the Mi Ultimo Adios.
