Out of the many creative outputs that Rizal made in his lifetime, the story of the Monkey and the Tortoise is one of his distinct works. Not only did Rizal write the story in English, but it was also sketched to look like a comic strip. It was supposed to be just a banter to his pal and Juan Luna’s wife, Paz Pardo de Tavera, when she asked Rizal to write in her autograph album before the pages ran out (Craig 1913, 160), but it turned out to be something more philosophical than just a simple children’s story.

Rizal did not just retell the famous fable, but also thought about its resemblance with another fable from Japan, and wrote an in-depth analysis that was published in the Trübner’s Record in 1889. He even mentioned that this idea might ‘be of some interest to ethnologists,’ giving us an idea of his fascination with different cultures, especially the culture in the Philippines. He highlighted the difference of animals used as the main character in the story: tortoise for the Philippine version and crab from the Japanese version. Both animals symbolize a belief of its respective culture, drawn from common sayings and customs. This is also true with the plants used in the story: a banana tree, which is abundant in the Philippines, and persimmon-seed and rice cake, which are possibly more common in Japan. His critical thinking was not only reflected through his socio-political endeavors but also in topics such as anthropology, which exhibits his firm connection to his heritage and endless curiosity. Nevertheless, towards the end of his article, he still emphasized that both stories hold a ‘great deal of morality: it is the eternal fight between the weak and the powerful.’ Considering the origins of the stories may have come long before the European expeditions and there was a pre-existing power dynamic between social classes. In the case of the Philippines and Spain, it was a colony-colonizer kind of dynamic that shaped how Filipinos acted, adapted, and accepted the realities of that time. Ultimately, it is the wisdom and values that the tortoise possesses that made her survive in the story, just as a Filipino would have done in the daily life of a colonized nation.

While this fable is merely a retelling of an old Philippine folktale which he also described to be known everywhere in the country and in the ‘Far East,’ it is nice to think of Rizal, the national hero, as a cartoonist or sketch artist in his free time which makes him more relatable like an ordinary person. This short fable, written years after the two most famous novels, also shows how good and considerate of a storyteller Jose Rizal was, even illustrating the story to make it easy for children to digest. If Rizal had not been a doctor, he probably would have become either an ethnographer, or an artist (of prose, poetry, fine art, name it).
