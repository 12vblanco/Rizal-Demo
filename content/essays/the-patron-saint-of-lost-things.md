When tracing back the narrative of the infiltration of Spanish colonial rule, it has always been described as successful through the reduccion, a Spanish colonial policy that establishes the church at the center of the town and “civilized society”. This was made possible through the decree of King Phillip of Spain, who assigned religious orders to certain territories to preach the Catholic faith. They were tasked to establish pueblos, and all new Christian converts and unbaptized individuals were required to build their houses around the church.

In Laguna de Bai (now Laguna), it began in 1578 with the arrival of Franciscan missionaries, who worked in pairs. Upon receiving permission to move out of Manila, Fray Juan de Plascencia and Fray Diego de Oropesa started their mission, which was deemed successful with the establishment of pueblos along the lake areas of Lumbang, Baras, Morong, Siniloan, Pangil, Paete, Majayjay, Nagcarlan, Pililia, and Pila between 1578 and 1595. Relating to the people might have been appealing, having Fray Plascencia’s fluency in Tagalog and even translating the Doctrina Cristiana to the catechumens. He also wrote the Relacion de las Costumbres de los Tagalogs, whose sole purpose was to serve as a guide to other missionaries and prevent abuses against the people.

It is worth noting, however, that the establishment of Franciscan missions in these pueblos was proven to be effective through the collaboration of the principalia class. They are regarded as initiators of faith through their financial support for church construction and embellishment of church icons. They even served as the purveyors of Christian morals, so that they could censure the parish priest for alleged immorality and violating the Christian principles of conduct.

Such was the case with Rizal’s family, through the influence of their mother, Teodora Alonzo, a celadora (laywoman) of the Franciscan Third Order in Calamba. She carried out her duty as the eldest sister of their group by upholding the faithful observance of the Rule, encouraging fraternity among her members, and ensuring that no sister suffered any material or spiritual abandonment in illness.

Perhaps Rizal’s Franciscan influence is best seen in his statuette of San Antonio de Padua, made during his exile in Dapitan, a time when he indulged himself in creative expression. Although some records state that the piece was completed by Biñan master sculptor and Rizal’s teacher, Romualdo de Jesus.

San Antonio de Padua is a notable saint in the Franciscan order, who lived around the time of the namesake of their order, San Francisco de Assisi. San Antonio was well-known for his talent and knowledge in preaching that in 1224, San Francisco wrote to him, asking him to teach theology to Franciscan friars. He also had a reputation as a miracle worker. One legend has it that when his precious psalter, which contained notes for preaching, was stolen, he prayed fervently for its return, and his prayer was granted. This resulted in him being known as the Patron Saint of Lost Things.

After his canonization as a saint, artists and sculptors created numerous images of him. He is sometimes depicted holding a book and a lily or a torch, preaching to fish while holding a monstrance with the Blessed Sacrament, or preaching in public or from a nut tree. But by the 17th century, the common iconography of San Antonio depicts him wearing a brown/gray habit, which represents poverty and humility. It also has a rope tied to its waist with three knots symbolizing the Trinity, while holding the Infant Jesus. This was based on the vision of Count Tiso, a politician who invited San Antonio to a hermitage in Camposampiero, Italy to recover from his illness. One night, Count Tiso passed by San Antonio’s room and saw a ray of light coming from his door. Curious, he approached the door and saw him kneeling and admiring a loving child of rare beauty who was tenderly embracing the saint.

This was the same image made by Rizal; however, ironically, the patron saint of lost things lost its head along with that of the infant Jesus’ during the Liberation of Manila in 1945. Thanks to the postcard produced by the Liberia Manila Filatélica in 1911, the image can be appreciated. It was not until 10 May 1948 when Trinidad Rizal donated it to Dr. Leoncio Lopez - Rizal Bureau of Public Libraries. At present, it is safely stored in the confines of the Museo ni Jose Rizal in Fort Santiago, and is put out for public viewing only during the commemoration of special occasions, such as his martyrdom.
