There is a tendency to remember José Rizal primarily through his novels, especially Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo. Yet in 1886, while in Germany and in the final stages of revising the Noli, Rizal was immersed in a different kind of labor: translating the Wilhelm Tell by Friedrich Schiller into Tagalog, which he titled Guillermo Tell. This translation, often treated as a minor footnote, reveals a more deliberate and complex engagement with language, readership, and political imagination.

Rizal’s decision to translate Wilhelm Tell did not emerge from abstraction. In a letter to Ferdinand Blumentritt, he explained that the request came from his compatriots, including his brother Paciano. The task, however, proved far from simple. German and Tagalog, he noted, differ not only in vocabulary but in their very structure of thought. He described having to dwell on each phrase, to “think it over” before attempting a Tagalog equivalent, all while avoiding “germanisms” that would distort the flow of the target language. This careful attention to expression shaped the entire translation.

What emerges from his correspondence is a clear philosophy of translation. Rizal did not aim for rigid, word-for-word accuracy. Instead, he pursued what he understood as the “sense” of the phrase. When a direct equivalent could not be found, he allowed the translation to become more flexible, provided that it did not depart from the meaning. This approach can be seen in how he handled specific terms. For instance, the German word Vaterland was rendered as amang-lupa, a phrase that does not replicate the original term but evokes a similar emotional and cultural resonance. Here, Rizal privileges intelligibility and affect over literal correspondence.

Another example appears in his handling of political vocabulary. The phrase “inalienable rights,” central to Enlightenment discourse, does not appear in the Tagalog text in its expected form. Instead, Rizal translates it as “ang di matingkalang katuiran” or “incomprehensible reason.” The shift is subtle but significant. Rather than foregrounding legal or philosophical abstraction, the translation moves toward a more experiential register, one that resonates with moral and spiritual understanding. In a similar way, the German Freiheit (freedom) is at times translated as kalayaan, but elsewhere appears as kaligtasan (salvation). These choices suggest that Rizal was not merely translating language but reframing concepts to fit a different horizon of meaning.

This strategy becomes even clearer when we consider Rizal’s method. His process can be understood in three stages. First, he sought to grasp the meaning of the phrase within German. Second, he searched for a corresponding expression in Tagalog. Third, he refined this phrasing so that it would read naturally, free from foreign structures. The result is a text that does not sound like a translation. Instead, it reads as if the story had always belonged to the language.

At the same time, Rizal’s translation cannot be separated from its historical moment. Wilhelm Tell is a drama of resistance, centered on the right to oppose tyranny when oppression becomes unbearable. Rizal encountered this text while his own political thinking was shifting. The act of translating it into Tagalog allowed him to carry that narrative into a different context. It is important to note that his novels, written in Spanish, circulated mainly among the ilustrado class. Guillermo Tell, on the other hand, was directed toward a broader Tagalog-speaking audience.

This intention is evident in the form of the translation. Rizal did not retain Schiller’s structure entirely. Instead, he rendered most of the text into prose while transforming selected passages into Tagalog verse forms such as the kundiman. These were familiar to Filipino audiences and widely used in oral traditions. By doing so, Rizal made the text more accessible, not only to readers but also to listeners. The translation could be recited, performed, or even staged. ￼

There were also moments where Rizal also drew from existing Tagalog cultural frameworks. For example, the German term Vogt (a kind of governor or steward) is translated as hukom, a word that also appears in the Tagalog pasyon referring to Pontius Pilate. This is not a neutral substitution. It aligns the figure of authority in Wilhelm Tell with a familiar image of unjust judgment, thereby deepening its resonance for a local audience. Through such choices, Rizal situates the narrative within a moral universe already understood by his readers.

Yet the translation also reveals tensions. Rizal himself admitted that he no longer used Tagalog regularly during his years in Europe. His brother Paciano later observed that while the translation was acceptable, some parts were difficult to understand despite being correct. This suggests that Rizal was working at the limits of his linguistic comfort, negotiating between fluency and distance. The effort itself becomes part of the text’s significance.

The reception of Guillermo Tell remains uncertain. There is no clear record that it was widely read or performed during Rizal’s lifetime. The manuscript circulated among a small circle, and it was only published years after his death. Still, its importance lies less in immediate impact than in what it reveals about Rizal’s intellectual trajectory. It shows a moment when he turned toward the vernacular, not simply as a medium, but as a space where political ideas could take root more widely.

Rizal’s translation of Wilhelm Tell invites us to see him differently. He is not only a novelist or a reformist thinker. He is also a translator who understood that language shapes how ideas are received. Through Guillermo Tell, he was not just introducing Schiller to Filipino readers. He was testing how a story of resistance could be retold in a way that felt immediate, familiar, and potentially transformative.
