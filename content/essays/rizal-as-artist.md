Despite their designation as National Cultural Treasures, José Rizal's artworks often receive less attention than his many other accomplishments. Yet one cannot separate the artist from the hero who, by age five, was already modeling clay and wax, carving wood, and learning to write. Raised in an environment that nurtured imagination, young Rizal created his own maquettes, a practice he carried into his years in Dapitan, where he made objects that were both functional and playful.

His surviving works—portraits, busts, a European travel sketch, and the San Pablo el Ermitaño sculpture created for his Jesuit mentor Fr. Pablo Pastells—form a quiet narrative of his life. Displayed today at the National Museum of the Philippines, these pieces reveal aspects of Rizal's inner world, including the introspection and spirituality that marked his years in exile.

![Rizal's clay sculpture of San Pablo el Ermitaño: the bearded hermit reclining against a rock, one arm raised, the base inscribed BEATVS PAVLVS PRIMVS EREMITA](san-pablo-el-ermitano/front.webp "Figure 1.0 San Pablo el Ermitaño")

Rizal's art also reflects his deep connection to Dapitan, the town he devoted himself to improving. Among these works are a topographical map of the area made with Fr. Francisco Sanchez; Oyang Dapitana, a clay sculpture of a local woman washing clothes; and Josephine Sleeping, a tender sculpture of his last love, Josephine Bracken.

Throughout his life, art served both as a discipline and as an expression of his sentimiento nacional (national sentiment). He admired Juan Luna's Spoliarium for its nationalist force, even within its European setting.

![Rizal's clay group La Venganza de la Madre: a mother dog biting into the back of a crocodile that has seized her pup, the base signed RIZAL DAPITAN 1894](la-venganza-de-la-madre/front.webp "Figure 2.0 La Venganza de la Madre (The Mother's Revenge)")

Rizal also employed allegory to express political ideas, as seen in La Venganza de la Madre (The Mother's Revenge), where Inang Bayan (the motherland), depicted as a dog rescuing her pup from a crocodile, symbolizes resistance to Spanish oppression. His Prometheus Bound, drawn from Greek myth, depicts the titan punished for giving humans fire—the first technology. In this work, Rizal reflects on the costs of enlightenment and the limits imposed on those who challenge power.
