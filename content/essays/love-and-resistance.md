La Venganza de la Madre is a terracotta sculpture depicting a mother dog sinking its teeth into a crocodile with a puppy in its jaws. The inspiration came from a real incident when José Rizal's students ignored his advice and took a boat ride to central Dapitan. A puppy named Syria followed them to the water and was captured by a crocodile. Rizal created this sculpture to teach his students a lesson about the consequences of disobedience (Craig 1913). He viewed moral education as integral to civic education, as reflected in his curriculum and in the school song “Hymn to Talisay.”

Historian Gregorio Zaide documents in his book Jose Rizal: Asia's First Apostle of Nationalism that the sculpture was then interpreted as an allegory of the inevitability of resistance to oppression, despite the physical disadvantage of the mother dog. According to Zaide (1970), the mother dog represents Inang Bayan (Motherland), while the puppy and the crocodile symbolize the Filipino people and Spain, respectively. Further, in Rizal’s own writings, he used the crocodile to represent the friars in the Philippines (Craig 1909).

While this allegory has been embraced, Rizal did not support violent resistance. He reinforced his advocacy for moral education in El Filibusterismo, where Padre Florentino told Simoun that his revolution failed because he did not “sow ideas” in place of the societal ills he wanted to purge through anarchy.

Since then, the sculpture’s reinterpretation as a nationalist symbol has sustained continuing influence. In 2008, La Venganza de la Madre was declared a National Cultural Treasure (NCT) by the National Museum of the Philippines for its "outstanding historical, cultural, and artistic value." Meanwhile, modern analyses of the artwork’s tropes take on new meaning in the postcolonial Philippines. Most popular is how the crocodile has transformed from the colonizer into a symbol of corrupt public officials whose greed cannot be satisfied, and prey primarily on the weak and most unlikely to be able to stand up for themselves.

The depiction of the mother dog, on the other hand, is intentionally exaggerated to emphasize her emotional strength. E. San Juan, Jr., in his book Sisa’s Vengeance: Jose Rizal’s Sexual Politics and Cultural Revolution, positions one of the most unjust casualties in Rizal’s Noli Me Tangere to embody the vengeful matriarch trope in the character of Sisa. With no protection from the state against its own abuses and neglect, San Juan uses the term “patriarchal nihilism” to describe the colonial system that is “bereft of god/nomos” (law or moral order) and that dehumanizes Sisa as “a non-entity,” "sensuous psyche,” “inert object,” and “beast” when she was driven mad (San Juan 2011).

Yet, even through her tragic ending, Rizal gave voice to the marginalized through this true-to-life representation of Sisa as just one of the many we have lost to abuse. Amidst the main actors in his novels, Rizal used the weakest link to confront the abuse of power, but also to reveal the true weakness: failing to win liberty due to lack of bravery to suffer for Sisa; writing in his diary on 31 March 1884, “Liberty is a woman who grants her favors only to the brave. Enslaved peoples have to suffer much to win her, and those who abuse her lose her.” And in 1890, Rizal wrote a warning in The Philippines a Century Hence: “Every country gets the fate it deserves.”

Finally, sympathy for the puppy, especially the underdog, is a common trope in local films (Tiongson 1983), owing to the series of different colonizers in the country. We may aspire to be the avenging Inang Bayan (Motherland), but we habitually identify with the underdog; upholding the value of resilience, and withstanding challenges by yielding. Yet over time, this brand of resilience has proven more romantic than realistic, leading some to praise the resilience of the impoverished rather than truly empowering them.

In his book Culture and History, Nick Joaquin critiques what he calls a “heritage of smallness” that blames our lack of originality on colonizers hindering our progress and culture, as we are not the only country that has been colonized. He writes, “Rizal was surely more of a freeman than the unconquered tribesmen up in the hills, because the colonial years brought an influx of new ideas and pushed us towards the larger effort of advancing freedom and unifying the land… If we have a "heritage of greatness," it's in these labors and in the three epic acts of the colonial period: first, the defense of the land during two centuries of siege; second, the Propaganda Movement; and third, the Revolution” (Joaquin 1983).

While the La Venganza de la Madre asserts the inevitability of resistance, Joaquin suggests we consider the inadvertent positive role of the colonizer in forcing the Filipino not to outsource self-determination, which challenges the black-and-white view between oppressor vs. oppressed.
