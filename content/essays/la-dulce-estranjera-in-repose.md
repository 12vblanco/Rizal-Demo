“If Rizal can be said to have ever loved anyone else but the Filipino Nation, then it must be said that he loved Josephine; he was her husband twice over, her open lover in defiance of all his innate propriety and sensibility; she was the one woman with whom he shared that most jealously prized of all his possessions, his name, and also his heart’s intimacies.”

- Leon Ma. Guerrero, The First Filipino: A Biography of Jose Rizal (1963, 301)

Josephine Sleeping, dubbed as “Rizal’s Mona Lisa,” is a tribute to Rizal’s last love, a “slender, brown hair and blue-eyed Irish girl,” his common-law wife, Josephine Bracken. She is captured in this miniature clay sculpture—personally signed by Rizal—laying unperturbed on a divan. The portrayal is at once tender and sensual, with her nude upper body exposed from the sheets, and her right arm modestly covering one of her breasts.

In February 1895, Rizal met Josephine in Dapitan when her adoptive father, George Taufer, came to seek eye treatment. Rizal asked for Josephine’s hand in marriage, yet Taufer opposed for several reasons: he was jealously protective of his adoptive daughter after his wife’s death, was enraged upon Rizal's diagnosis that his blindness was incurable, and well aware that Rizal was a political exile. With no blessing from Taufer or the Catholic Church due to Rizal’s membership among the freemasons, Josephine lived together with Rizal regardless.

Some sources believe Rizal married Josephine in his death row cell in Fort Santiago, a few hours before his execution at Bagumbayan on December 30, 1896, making Rizal, as Guerrero writes, Josephine’s “husband twice over,” first as a de facto husband, and second as a sacramental union. However, others doubt the legitimacy of this account as it would imply Rizal’s retraction of his anti-clerical writings to gain permission from the Church to marry Josephine.

Yet having fought for the right to their union, the image of Josephine Sleeping comes as a chance snapshot of the other side of Rizal and Josephine’s unwavering determination not to let anything come against them: one of total abandon, even if their time together was cut short upon Rizal’s death.

For many decades, the statuette remained under the care of Rizal’s sister, Narcisa Rizal, making occasional appearances in publications and exhibitions, including a pictorial album on Rizal published by the José Rizal National Centennial Commission in 1961 (GMA Integrated News). In November 2024, the family heirloom re-entered public attention when it was offered at the León Gallery during the “Kingly Treasures Auction.” Bidders nationwide set their price, until it was finally sold for more than ₱31 million, setting a record for the most expensive artwork created by Rizal (Yap 2024).

Yet the story did not end with the record-breaker as the sole bidder present at Leon Gallery, Director General Jeremy Barns, invoked the National Museum of the Philippines’ legal right of first refusal under Republic Act 11333, allowing it to match the winning bid and acquire the sculpture for the National Fine Arts Collection (GMA Integrated News, “National Museum Acquires Jose Rizal’s Josephine Sleeping’”). This acquisition secures the statuette’s rightful place in the collection of the National Museum of Fine Arts, alongside other artworks by Rizal.
